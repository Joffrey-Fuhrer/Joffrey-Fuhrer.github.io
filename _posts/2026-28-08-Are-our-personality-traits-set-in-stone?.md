  <h2 style="text-align: center; font-variant: small-caps; color: #ffffff;">Are our personality traits set in stone, or can they change?</h2>

  <p style="font-size: 1.1rem; line-height: 1.7; color: #ffffff;">
    Personality has long fascinated me, and it is probably one of my favorite topics. What could be a deeper question than what makes us the way we are?
  </p>

  <p style="font-size: 1.1rem; line-height: 1.7; color: #ffffff;">
    The way personality is understood today is that five broad traits describe how people typically think, feel, and behave: agreeableness, conscientiousness, extraversion, neuroticism, and openness to experience. But an important question remains: are these traits relatively fixed, or do they change throughout our lives?
  </p>

  <p style="font-size: 1.1rem; line-height: 1.7; color: #ffffff;">
    A fascinating 2026 meta-analysis, combining data from 229 longitudinal studies involving more than 200,000 participants, examined how personality changes throughout life. The authors looked at several different aspects of personality change. Here, I will focus on two of them: mean-level change and changes in variance.
  </p>

  <p style="font-size: 1.1rem; line-height: 1.7; color: #ffffff;">
    <strong>Mean-level change</strong> asks whether the average level of a personality trait in a population changes over time. For example, does the average person become more conscientious or agreeable with age?
  </p>

  <p style="font-size: 1.1rem; line-height: 1.7; color: #ffffff;">
    The answer is yes. Agreeableness and conscientiousness tend to increase during young adulthood, while neuroticism tends to decrease. Openness to experience and extraversion, in contrast, tend to increase throughout childhood and adolescence before gradually declining during adulthood.
  </p>

  <p style="font-size: 1.1rem; line-height: 1.7; color: #ffffff;">
    <strong>Changes in variance</strong> ask a different question: do people differ from one another in how much their personality changes? For example, some people may change considerably over time, while others remain relatively stable. This creates individual differences in personality change.
  </p>

  <p style="font-size: 1.1rem; line-height: 1.7; color: #ffffff;">
    According to this meta-analysis, there are significant individual differences in personality change for all five traits, with the largest differences observed for neuroticism. Agreeableness and openness to experience show the smallest individual differences. Interestingly, change is greatest during childhood and then gradually decreases, reaching a plateau somewhere between ages 30 and 75 depending on the trait. After that, personality remains relatively stable, although change never disappears completely.
  </p>

  <p style="font-size: 1.1rem; line-height: 1.7; color: #ffffff;">
    So, are our personalities set in stone? Clearly not. Personality changes across the lifespan, but the extent and direction of that change depend on the trait and on our stage of life. At the same time, people differ considerably in how much they change, suggesting that understanding personality development requires more than simply describing average trajectories.
  </p>

  <p style="font-size: 1.1rem; line-height: 1.7; color: #ffffff;">
    The next step for personality science will be to develop a compelling theory capable of explaining these different patterns of change. And that, I think, is the beginning of an exciting odyssey!
  </p>
